# Neutering or spaying your rabbit: why and when

Spaying (females) or neutering (males) your rabbit is not just about preventing
litters: it is first and foremost a **health** and **welfare** decision.

## Why it matters

- **In females**, the risk of **uterine cancer** rises with age: depending on the
  study, it may affect **around 60 to 80%** of unspayed females, mostly **after age
  2**. Spaying also prevents womb infections and false pregnancies.
- **In males**, neutering removes the risk of testicular cancer and reduces urine
  marking.
- **For behaviour**: less aggression, better litter habits, and the chance to
  **keep two rabbits together** without breeding.

## At what age

- **Males**: as soon as the testicles have descended, often around **3 to 4
  months**.
- **Females**: usually from **5 to 6 months** (a little later for giant breeds).
  For the anti-cancer benefit, it is best done **before age 2**.

## One thing not to forget

After the operation, **a male stays fertile for about 6 weeks** (a female is no
longer fertile straight away). So keep a recently neutered male away from unspayed
females during that period.

## What about the risk?

Rabbits are more sensitive to anaesthetic than cats or dogs, but with a
**rabbit-savvy vet** and modern techniques the procedure is routine. Recovery
usually takes **about 2 days for males** and **5 to 7 days for females**. Offer hay
as soon as your rabbit comes home: it should **start eating again quickly**, if
not, contact the vet again.

Talk it through with a vet who knows rabbits: they will advise the right time for
your animal.
